Mini exam 60 min: Part II (1)

Time 60 min, at most 21 points. Hints, feedback and model answers open when you end the exam.

  • Choose 1 of the 2 tasks before you answer.

1. FY1 · 2/3 · The fitted line 2 points

A student fitted a straight line to eight measured points. The line passes between the points but through none of them. Which interpretation is best?

  • A small scatter of the points around the line is to be expected. A measurement is repeated only if a point differs clearly and a reason for it is found. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • A zigzag line repeats the measurement errors and is not a model. The fitted line shows the relationship, and its slope can be read from it. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • A good fit runs through the middle of the points, so that there are points on both sides of it. A line forced through two points may describe the whole data set worse. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

  • Correct. The fit describes the model, and the small scatter of the points on both sides of it is random variation of the measurement. The line may pass through a point, but it does not have to. Review this in the chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line.

2. The kick scooter 2 points

The graph shows the velocity of a kick scooter. How far does the scooter travel during the time shown in the graph? Answer in metres.

m
Hint 1: a guiding question

Which region of the graph corresponds to the distance travelled?

Hint 2: what is the connection

The region is a trapezium: you can divide it into a rectangle and a triangle or calculate it with the average velocity.

Hint 3: an intermediate step
$\Delta x$$= \dfrac{2.0\,\text{m/s} + 7.0\,\text{m/s}}{2} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s}$
Show the solution

Given

from the graph, the initial velocity $v_0 = 2.0\,\text{m/s}$, the final velocity $v = 7.0\,\text{m/s}$, the time $t = 5.0\,\text{s}$; the velocity grows uniformly

Find

the distance travelled $\Delta x$ in metres

Principle

The scooter moves in one direction, so the distance is the displacement, and the displacement is the area under the $(t,v)$ graph (chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion). The velocity grows uniformly, so the region is a trapezium.

Reading the graph

The line starts at the point $(0$; $2.0\,\text{m/s})$ and ends at the point $(5.0\,\text{s}$; $7.0\,\text{m/s})$.

Formula

In uniformly accelerated motion $\Delta x = v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$ and $v = v_0 + a\,t$. Substituting $a\,t = v - v_0$ gives

$\Delta x$$= v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,(v - v_0)\,t$
$= \dfrac{v_0 + v}{2}\,t$

which is the area of the trapezium.

Conversions

The velocities are in $\text{m/s}$ and the time in seconds, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$\Delta x$$= \dfrac{2.0\,\text{m/s} + 7.0\,\text{m/s}}{2} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s}$
$= 4.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s}$
$= 22.5\,\text{m}$

Answer

$23\,\text{m}$

Two significant figures like the velocities and the time read from the graph.

Reasonableness

The result lies between the rectangles $2.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s} = 10\,\text{m}$ and $7.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s} = 35\,\text{m}$, as it must. By dividing: the rectangle $10\,\text{m}$ and the triangle $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 5.0\,\text{m/s} \cdot 5.0\,\text{s} = 12.5\,\text{m}$, in total the same $22.5\,\text{m}$.

3. Find the mistake: the power of a series resistor 2 points

Resistors $R_1 = 15\,\Omega$ and $R_2 = 30\,\Omega$ are connected in series to a $9.0\,\text{V}$ voltage source. A student calculated the power of the resistor $R_2$: “$P_2 = \tfrac{U^2}{R_2} = \tfrac{(9.0\,\text{V})^2}{30\,\Omega} = 2.7\,\text{W}$.” What is wrong in the solution?

  • In series the source voltage is shared between the resistors, $U = U_1 + U_2$. The voltage across the resistor $R_2$ is $6.0\,\text{V}$ and across the resistor $R_1$ $3.0\,\text{V}$. Review the chapter Kirchhoff's laws and potential in a circuit.

  • Both forms work when the quantities of this particular resistor are substituted in them. The mistake is using the source voltage: $U_2 = 6.0\,\text{V}$ gives the same $1.2\,\text{W}$ as $R_2 I^2$. Review the chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor.

  • Correct. The current is $I = \tfrac{9.0\,\text{V}}{45\,\Omega} = 0.20\,\text{A}$, so $U_2 = R_2 I = 6.0\,\text{V}$ and $P_2 = \tfrac{(6.0\,\text{V})^2}{30\,\Omega} = 1.2\,\text{W}$.

4. The wood chip power plant 15 points

Example data.

A town's combined heat and power plant burns forest chips and produces both electricity and district heat. On a frosty day the following were read from the screen in the plant's control room:

  • chips are fed into the boiler at $42\,\text{t}$ per hour
  • heat of combustion of the chips $9.5\,\text{MJ/kg}$
  • electrical power $30\,\text{MW}$
  • district heating power $70\,\text{MW}$

For comparison, the same electricity could be produced in a condensing power plant with an efficiency of $38\,\%$, and the same district heat in a boiler producing only heat, with an efficiency of $90\,\%$.

  1. a) How large is the fuel power of the plant? 4 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    Wood chips contain chemical energy, and they are fed into the boiler all the time. Which quantity tells how much energy arrives every second, and what do you need to calculate it?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    The energy of the burned chips is the heating value times the mass: $Q = Hm$. Power is energy divided by time: $P_0 = \dfrac{Q}{t}$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Convert the mass burned in an hour from tonnes to kilograms and the hour to seconds, $t = 3600\,\text{s}$. Then calculate the energy of one hour and divide it by the time.

  2. b) Calculate the electrical efficiency and the overall efficiency of the plant. 4 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    Efficiency tells what fraction of the fuel power is put to use. What counts as useful in the electrical efficiency, and what in the total efficiency?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    $\eta = \dfrac{P_\text{useful}}{P_0}$: for the electrical efficiency the useful part is the electric power $P_\text{el}$, and for the total efficiency the electric and district-heating power together,

    $\eta_\text{tot}$$= \dfrac{P_\text{el} + P_\text{dh}}{P_0}$
    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Use the fuel power of part a as the denominator. Calculate the total useful power $P_\text{el} + P_\text{dh}$ first and divide only then.

  3. c) How many tonnes of chips per hour would be needed if the same electricity and the same district heat were produced separately in a condensing power plant and a heating boiler? 4 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    In separate production each plant needs its own fuel. How do you find the fuel power when the useful power and the efficiency are known?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    The fuel power of each plant is $P_0 = \dfrac{P_\text{useful}}{\eta}$, and the powers are added together. The mass of chips is $m = \dfrac{P_0\,t}{H}$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    First calculate the fuel power of the condensing plant and of the heat boiler separately, and their sum. Then calculate the energy of one hour and the mass of chips, and convert it to tonnes. Compare the result with the $42\,\text{t}$ of the combined production.

  4. d) In summer district heat is needed only for hot tap water. Explain why the overall efficiency of the plant does not then stay the same if it produces the same electrical power. 3 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    Where does the heat of the condenser go in summer, when there is no need for district heating? Who makes use of it then?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    Only the power that ends up as useful is counted in the total efficiency:

    $\eta_\text{tot}$$= \dfrac{P_\text{el} + P_\text{dh}}{P_0}$

    What happens to the numerator when $P_\text{dh}$ gets smaller?

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Write the expression of the total efficiency for the case $P_\text{dh}$ small or zero and compare it with the efficiencies of part b. What should be considered in running the plant then?

Skills from the chapters: Fuels and heat of combustion $E = Hm$, Energy production and the efficiency of power plants, Power: $P = \Delta E/\Delta t$

Model answer

a)

Given

mass of chips burnt in an hour $m = 42\,\text{t}$, time $t = 1\,\text{h}$, heat of combustion $H = 9.5\,\text{MJ/kg}$

Find

the fuel power $P_0$ in megawatts

Principle

The energy released from the chips is the heat of combustion multiplied by the mass burnt, because the heat of combustion is energy per kilogram (chapter Fuels and heat of combustion). The fuel power is this energy divided by the time in which it is released (chapter Power).

Formula

$Q = Hm$ and $P_0 = \dfrac{Q}{t}$, so $P_0 = \dfrac{Hm}{t}$

Conversions

$42\,\text{t}$$= 42 \cdot 1000\,\text{kg}$
$= 42\,000\,\text{kg}$

$9.5\,\text{MJ/kg} = 9.5 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J/kg}$ and $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$; finally $1\,\text{W} = 10^{-6}\,\text{MW}$.

Substitution

$P_0$$= \dfrac{9.5 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J}/\text{kg} \cdot 42\,000\,\text{kg}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= \dfrac{3.99 \cdot 10^{11}\,\text{J}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= 1.108 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{W}$
$= 110.8\,\text{MW}$

Answer

$P_0 \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{2}\,\text{MW}$

Two significant figures like the heat of combustion $9.5\,\text{MJ/kg}$ and the mass $42\,\text{t}$. The later parts use the unrounded value $110.8\,\text{MW}$.

Reasonableness

The fuel power is a little greater than the sum of the electrical and district heating powers, $100\,\text{MW}$, as conservation of energy requires: the remaining $11\,\text{MW}$ or so is losses. With round numbers

$10\,\text{MJ/kg} \cdot 40\,000\,\text{kg} : 3600\,\text{s}$
$\approx 110\,\text{MW}$

b)

Given

from part a $P_0 = 110.8\,\text{MW}$; electrical power $P_\text{el} = 30\,\text{MW}$, district heating power $P_\text{dh} = 70\,\text{MW}$

Find

the electrical efficiency $\eta_\text{el}$ and the overall efficiency $\eta_\text{tot}$ as percentages

Principle

Efficiency is the fraction of the power taken in that is useful. In the electrical efficiency only electricity is useful, in the overall efficiency electricity and district heat together (chapter Energy production and the efficiency of power plants).

Formula

$\eta_\text{el}$$= \dfrac{P_\text{el}}{P_0}$
$\eta_\text{tot}$$= \dfrac{P_\text{el} + P_\text{dh}}{P_0}$

Conversions

All the powers are in megawatts, so the unit cancels. The ratio is converted to a percentage by multiplying it by $100\,\%$.

Substitution

$\eta_\text{el}$$= \dfrac{30\,\text{MW}}{110.8\,\text{MW}}$
$= 0.271$
$= 27.1\,\%$
$\eta_\text{tot}$$= \dfrac{30\,\text{MW} + 70\,\text{MW}}{110.8\,\text{MW}}$
$= 0.902$
$= 90.2\,\%$

Answer

$\eta_\text{el} \approx 27\,\%$

And $\eta_\text{tot} \approx 90\,\%$, two significant figures like the powers $30\,\text{MW}$ and $70\,\text{MW}$.

Reasonableness

Both are below $100\,\%$. The electrical efficiency is smaller than the roughly $40\,\%$ of an ordinary condensing power plant, because in combined heat and power the steam is condensed at a higher temperature for district heating. An overall efficiency of $90\,\%$ is typical of combined heat and power.

c)

Given

electrical power $P_\text{el} = 30\,\text{MW}$ and district heating power $P_\text{dh} = 70\,\text{MW}$; efficiency of the condensing power plant $\eta_\text{cond} = 38\,\%$, of the heating boiler $\eta_\text{hb} = 90\,\%$; $H = 9.5\,\text{MJ/kg}$; time $t = 1\,\text{h}$

Find

the mass $m$ of chips needed per hour in separate production, in tonnes

Principle

Each plant needs a fuel power that is its useful power divided by its efficiency. The fuel powers of the plants are added up, and the mass is obtained from the energy of one hour with the heat of combustion as in part a.

Formula

$P_0$$= \dfrac{P_\text{el}}{\eta_\text{cond}} + \dfrac{P_\text{dh}}{\eta_\text{hb}}$

$P_0\,t = Hm$, so $m = \dfrac{P_0\,t}{H}$

Conversions

$38\,\% = 0.38$, $90\,\% = 0.90$, $1\,\text{MW} = 10^{6}\,\text{W}$, $1\,\text{h} = 3600\,\text{s}$, $9.5\,\text{MJ/kg} = 9.5 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J/kg}$ and finally $1\,\text{kg} = 0.001\,\text{t}$.

Substitution

$P_0$$= \dfrac{30\,\text{MW}}{0.38} + \dfrac{70\,\text{MW}}{0.90}$
$= 78.9\,\text{MW} + 77.8\,\text{MW}$
$= 156.7\,\text{MW}$
$m$$= \dfrac{156.7 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{W} \cdot 3600\,\text{s}}{9.5 \cdot 10^{6}\,\text{J}/\text{kg}}$
$= 59\,390\,\text{kg}$
$= 59.4\,\text{t}$

Answer

about $59\,\text{t}$ of chips per hour, two significant figures like the powers and the heat of combustion. That is about $17\,\text{t}$ more than the $42\,\text{t}$ of combined heat and power.

Reasonableness

Separate production needs more fuel, because the heat of the condensing power plant's condenser is wasted. Combined heat and power saves $1 - 42 : 59.4 \approx 0.29$, that is, about a third of the fuel, which is a typical order of magnitude for combined heat and power.

d)

Find

why the overall efficiency falls in summer, when little district heat is needed

Principle

Efficiency depends on what is counted as useful (chapter Efficiency). A heat engine cannot turn all heat into electricity; part must be given off to the colder surroundings (chapter Energy production and the efficiency of power plants).

Model answer

Heat must in any case be removed from the steam cycle in the condenser when the plant produces electricity. In winter this heat warms district heating water and counts as useful, so the overall efficiency is about $90\,\%$. In summer district heat is needed only for tap water, so most of the condenser's heat must be given off to the surroundings, for example to cooling water, as waste heat.

If the electrical power stays at $30\,\text{MW}$, about the same amount of fuel is still used, but less of it is useful, and the overall efficiency falls towards the electrical efficiency. That is why a combined heat and power plant is run according to the heat demand: in summer at a lower power or not at all, and the electricity is produced elsewhere.

Common mistakes

  • The tonnes per hour are divided directly by the hour without conversions, so the power is obtained in the unit $\text{MJ/h}$ and compared with megawatts.
  • Only electricity is counted in the overall efficiency, or the efficiency is calculated from the electrical power ($70 : 30$).
  • In part c the electrical and heating powers are added up and divided by a single efficiency, even though there are two plants.
  • In part c the efficiencies are multiplied together, even though the plants are not in series but in parallel.

Also accepted

  • In part a $P_0 \approx 111\,\text{MW}$ and the later parts calculated with it: $\eta_\text{el} \approx 27\,\%$ and $\eta_\text{tot} \approx 90\,\%$.

5. A guitar string and tuning 15 points

Example data.

The vibrating part of the A string of a guitar, from the nut to the bridge, is $648\,\text{mm}$, and played open the string sounds at its fundamental frequency $110.0\,\text{Hz}$. When the string is pressed against a fret, the vibrating part of the string reaches from the fret to the bridge. The guitarist tunes the string against the note A of a keyboard, whose frequency is $110.0\,\text{Hz}$.

  • speed of sound in room air $343\,\text{m/s}$
  • the tension of the string stays the same in parts a and b
  1. a) At what speed does a wave travel along the string? How long is the wavelength of the fundamental in the string and in the sound leaving the guitar through the air? 4 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    Are the ends of the string nodes or antinodes? (An antinode is where the string moves the most.) What fraction of a wavelength is the length of the string when it vibrates at its fundamental frequency?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    At the fundamental the length of the string is half a wavelength: $\lambda_1 = 2L$. The speed of the wave is $v = f_1 \lambda_1$. In air the frequency stays the same, but the wavelength depends on the speed of sound in air.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    First calculate the wavelength $\lambda_1$ of the string in metres and substitute it into the formula for speed. For the wavelength in air use the speed of sound $343\,\text{m/s}$ and the same frequency.

  2. b) How long must the vibrating part of the string be for the string to sound the note B, $123.5\,\text{Hz}$? How far from the nut is the fret? 4 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    What stays the same when the string is pressed against a fret, and what changes? What follows for the speed of the wave?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    The string and its tension do not change, so the speed $v$ of the wave is the same as in part a. The fundamental frequency of the shortened part is $f' = \dfrac{v}{2L'}$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Solve the shortened vibrating length $L'$ in symbols before any numbers. The distance of the fret from the nut is the difference between the original and the shortened length. Convert the answer to millimetres at the end.

  3. c) When the string and the keyboard note sound at the same time, the guitarist hears $6$ beats in $4.0$ seconds. They tighten the string slightly, and the beats become slower. What was the frequency of the string before it was tightened? 4 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    What does beating tell you about the difference between two nearly equal frequencies? How is the number of beats per unit time related to this difference?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    The beat frequency is the absolute value of the difference of the frequencies: $f_\text{h} = |f_\text{string} - 110.0\,\text{Hz}|$. The beat frequency is the number of beats divided by the time.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    First calculate $f_\text{h}$. The absolute value gives two possible frequencies for the string. What does tightening do to the frequency of the string? Which of the two options fits the beats becoming slower?

  4. d) The guitarist lightly touches the open, sounding string exactly at its midpoint, and the string goes on sounding an octave higher. Explain why this happens. 3 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    What does the light touch of a finger force to happen at the midpoint of the string? What modes of vibration can the string be left with?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    The string can only have modes of vibration with a node at each end, with natural frequencies $f_n = n f_1$. The touch of the finger adds a node at the midpoint as well.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Sketch the modes $n = 1$, $n = 2$ and $n = 3$ and see which of them have a node at the midpoint. Which is the lowest, and how does its frequency compare with the fundamental frequency?

Skills from the chapters: Standing waves: nodes, antinodes and natural frequencies, How waves form and travel, Sound as a wave: pitch, timbre and the Doppler effect

Model answer

a)

Given

length of the vibrating part of the string $L = 648\,\text{mm}$, fundamental frequency $f_1 = 110.0\,\text{Hz}$, speed of sound in air $v_\text{air} = 343\,\text{m/s}$

Find

the speed $v$ of the wave in the string and the wavelength of the fundamental in the string $\lambda_1$ and in air $\lambda_\text{air}$

Principle

The string is fixed at the nut and at the bridge, so there are nodes at the ends. At the fundamental frequency the string has one antinode, and the length of the string is half the wavelength (chapter Standing waves: nodes, antinodes and natural frequencies). The string makes the air vibrate at the same frequency, but sound travels in air at a different speed from the wave in the string, so the wavelength is different (chapter How waves form and travel).

Formula

$L = \dfrac{\lambda_1}{2}$, so $\lambda_1 = 2L$ and $v = f_1\lambda_1 = 2Lf_1$; in air

$\lambda_\text{air}$$= \dfrac{v_\text{air}}{f_1}$

Conversions

$648\,\text{mm}$$= 648 \cdot 0.001\,\text{m}$
$= 0.648\,\text{m}$

Substitution

$\lambda_1$$= 2 \cdot 0.648\,\text{m}$
$= 1.296\,\text{m}$
$v$$= 110.0\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}} \cdot 1.296\,\text{m}$
$= 142.56\,\text{m/s}$
$\lambda_\text{air}$$= \dfrac{343\,\text{m/s}}{110.0\,\text{Hz}}$
$= 3.118\,\text{m}$

Answer

The wave travels along the string at the speed $v \approx 143\,\text{m/s}$. The wavelength of the fundamental is $1.30\,\text{m}$ in the string and $3.12\,\text{m}$ in air, three significant figures like the speed of sound $343\,\text{m/s}$ and the length $648\,\text{mm}$.

Reasonableness

The frequency is the same in both, so the ratio of the wavelengths is the ratio of the speeds:

$3.118 : 1.296$$\approx 2.41$
$= 343 : 142.6$
$3.118 : 1.296$
$\approx 2.41$
$= 343 : 142.6$

The wavelength in the string is twice the length of the string, as it should be at the fundamental frequency.

b)

Given

speed of the wave in the string $v = 142.56\,\text{m/s}$ (part a), wanted frequency $f' = 123.5\,\text{Hz}$, full length $L = 648\,\text{mm}$

Find

the length $L'$ of the vibrating part of the string and the distance of the fret from the nut

Principle

The string and its tension stay the same, so the speed of the wave in the string does not change. The fret is a new node, so the part vibrating at the fundamental frequency is half the new wavelength (chapter Standing waves: nodes, antinodes and natural frequencies). The distance of the fret from the nut is the difference between the full length and the vibrating part.

Formula

$f' = \dfrac{v}{2L'}$, so $L' = \dfrac{v}{2f'}$, and the distance from the nut is $d = L - L' = L - \dfrac{v}{2f'}$

Conversions

Finally $1\,\text{m} = 1000\,\text{mm}$.

Substitution

$L'$$= \dfrac{142.56\,\text{m/s}}{2 \cdot 123.5\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}}$
$= 0.5772\,\text{m}$
$= 577.2\,\text{mm}$

and $$ \begin{aligned} 648\,\text{mm} - 577.2\,\text{mm} &= 70.8\,\text{mm} \end{aligned} $$$$ \begin{aligned} &648\,\text{mm} - 577.2\,\text{mm} \\ &= 70.8\,\text{mm} \end{aligned} $$

Answer

The vibrating part must be $L' \approx 577\,\text{mm}$, three significant figures like the given data, and the fret is about $71\,\text{mm}$ from the nut. The distance is a difference, so it is known to the nearest millimetre.

Reasonableness

The frequency grows by the factor $123.5 : 110.0 \approx 1.12$, so the length must become shorter by the same factor: $$ 648\,\text{mm} : 1.123 \approx 577\,\text{mm}\text{.}$$ On a guitar the second fret is about $7\,\text{cm}$ from the nut, so the result fits.

c)

Given

$6$ beats in $4.0$ seconds, reference note $110.0\,\text{Hz}$; tightening makes the beats slower

Find

the frequency of the string $f_\text{string}$ before tightening

Principle

The beat frequency of two sounds of almost the same frequency is the difference of the frequencies (chapter Sound as a wave: pitch, timbre and the Doppler effect).

The beats tell only the difference, not its direction, so the direction is deduced from the tightening: in a tighter string the wave travels faster, and the frequency $f = \tfrac{v}{2L}$ rises (chapter Standing waves: nodes, antinodes and natural frequencies). When the frequency rose and the beats became slower, the frequency difference became smaller, so the frequency of the string was below $110.0\,\text{Hz}$.

Formula

$f_\text{h} = \dfrac{N}{t}$ and $f_\text{h} = |f_\text{string} - f_\text{ref}|$, so $f_\text{string} = f_\text{ref} - f_\text{h}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$f_\text{h}$$= \dfrac{6}{4.0\,\text{s}}$
$= 1.5\,\text{Hz}$

so

$f_\text{string}$$= 110.0\,\text{Hz} - 1.5\,\text{Hz}$
$= 108.5\,\text{Hz}$

Answer

$f_\text{string} = 108.5\,\text{Hz}$

The precision is a tenth, because the reference note is known to $0.1\,\text{Hz}$ and the beats $1.5\,\text{Hz}$ to two figures.

Reasonableness

If the string had been at $111.5\,\text{Hz}$, tightening would have increased the difference and the beats would have become faster. The frequency difference $1.5\,\text{Hz}$ is below about $10\,\text{Hz}$, so the beats really can be heard.

d)

Find

why a string lightly touched at its midpoint sounds an octave higher

Principle

The natural frequencies of a string fixed at both ends are $f_n = n f_1$, and at the natural frequency $f_n$ the string has $n$ antinodes. The touch stops the motion of the string at that point, so a node forms there (chapter Standing waves: nodes, antinodes and natural frequencies).

Model answer

A freely sounding string vibrates at the fundamental frequency and at the overtones at the same time. At the fundamental frequency the midpoint is an antinode, so the finger damps the fundamental and all the odd natural frequencies, which have an antinode at the midpoint. What remains are the natural frequencies $n = 2, 4, 6, \ldots$, which have a node at the midpoint, because the finger does not stop their motion.

The lowest of them is $f_2 = 2f_1 = 220\,\text{Hz}$: the string vibrates in two segments, with an antinode in each half, and twice the frequency sounds an octave higher. The finger can be lifted, and the string still sounds the same way, because the standing wave goes on.

Common mistakes

  • In part a the length of the string is taken as the wavelength, so that $v = f_1 L \approx 71\,\text{m/s}$; at the fundamental frequency $L = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}$.
  • In part a the wavelength in the string $1.30\,\text{m}$ is given as the wavelength in air: the frequency stays the same, the wavelength does not.
  • In part b the speed is assumed to change, or the length is calculated as directly proportional to the frequency.
  • In part c the beat frequency is taken as $6\,\text{Hz}$, or both alternatives are given without justification.

Also accepted

  • In part b with a ratio: $$ \begin{aligned} L' &= L \cdot \dfrac{f_1}{f'} \\ &= 648\,\text{mm} \cdot \dfrac{110.0}{123.5} \\ &\approx 577\,\text{mm}\text{,} \end{aligned} $$ because the speed is the same.
