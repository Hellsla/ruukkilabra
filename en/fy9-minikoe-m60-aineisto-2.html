Mini exam 60 min: data task (2)

Time 60 min, at most 20 points. Hints, feedback and model answers open when you end the exam.

  • Choose 1 of the 2 tasks before you answer.

1. A local train arrives at the station 20 points

Example data.

A passenger recorded the velocity of a local train every four seconds with the positioning app of a phone as the train approached a station. The app gives the velocity in kilometres per hour as whole numbers. According to the manufacturer, the uncertainty of the displayed velocity is about $\pm 1\,\text{km/h}$.

$t\ (\text{s})$ $v\ (\text{km/h})$
$0$ $91$
$4$ $89$
$8$ $90$
$12$ $84$
$16$ $77$
$20$ $67$
$24$ $56$
$28$ $49$
$32$ $39$
$36$ $28$
$40$ $21$

The recording stopped at $40\,\text{s}$, when the train was still moving.

  1. a) Present the train's velocity as a function of time as a graph, and fit a suitable model to the points. 6 p
  2. b) Determine the train's acceleration during the braking. Give the answer in $\text{m/s}^2$. 5 p
  3. c) Use the graph to estimate when the train stops and how far it travels from the beginning of the braking until it stops. 5 p
  4. d) The passenger concludes that the train stopped exactly at the distance of part c from the beginning of the braking. Assess how reliable the conclusion is. 4 p

Skills from the chapters: Graphing and fitting a straight line, Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion, Measuring and the reliability of a measurement

Show the model answer and marking Open only when your own solution is ready.

Model answer

a)

Find

the train's velocity $v$ as a function of time $t$ as a graph, and a model fitted to it

Graph

On the horizontal axis is the time $t\ (\text{s})$ and on the vertical axis the velocity $v\ (\text{km/h})$. The time axis extends to about $55\,\text{s}$, so that the moment of stopping in part c fits in the graph.

Model answer

The points show two stages. At the moments $0$, $4$ and $8\,\text{s}$ the velocity stays at about $90\,\text{km/h}$: the variation of $\pm 1\,\text{km/h}$ is the size of the app's uncertainty, so the train moves uniformly, and a horizontal straight line is drawn for these points.

From $12\,\text{s}$ on, the velocity decreases by roughly the same amount every four seconds, so the train brakes uniformly, and a falling straight line is fitted to these points so that the points lie on both sides of it. The first three points are not included in the braking line, because they belong to a different stage and would tilt the line the wrong way. The falling line is extended as a dashed line to the time axis for part c.

b)

Given

the braking line of part a; the points $(12\,\text{s}$; $85\,\text{km/h})$ and $(40\,\text{s}$; $20\,\text{km/h})$ are read from it

Find

the acceleration during the braking $a$ in $\text{m/s}^2$

Principle

The braking points lie on a straight line, so the acceleration is constant and equal to the slope of the $(t,v)$ graph (chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion). The slope is read from the fitted line, from two points far apart, not from adjacent measured points, because every measurement has a small error (chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line).

Reading the graph

The line passes through the points $(12\,\text{s}$; $85\,\text{km/h})$ and $(40\,\text{s}$; $20\,\text{km/h})$, which are circled in the model graph.

Formula

$a$$= \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$
$= \dfrac{v_2 - v_1}{t_2 - t_1}$

Conversions

The change in velocity to metres per second by substituting the units:

$20\,\text{km/h} - 85\,\text{km/h}$$= -65\,\text{km/h}$
$= \dfrac{-65 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= -18.06\,\text{m/s}$
$20\,\text{km/h} - 85\,\text{km/h}$
$= -65\,\text{km/h}$
$= \dfrac{-65 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= -18.06\,\text{m/s}$

Substitution

$a$$= \dfrac{-18.06\,\text{m/s}}{40\,\text{s} - 12\,\text{s}}$
$= \dfrac{-18.06\,\text{m/s}}{28\,\text{s}}$
$= -0.645\,\text{m/s}^2$

Answer

$a \approx -0.65\,\text{m/s}^2$

Two significant figures, because the velocities read from the line are known to about two figures. Results of $-0.61$…$-0.68\,\text{m/s}^2$ are within the range of the fit.

Reasonableness

The acceleration is negative, that is, opposite to the velocity, so the speed decreases, as it must when braking. The magnitude $0.65\,\text{m/s}^2$ is a usual service braking of a train: the passengers stay on their feet. The spreadsheet fit gives $-2.31\,\text{km/h}$ per second, that is, $-0.642\,\text{m/s}^2$, almost the same.

c)

Given

the graph of part a: the braking begins at the crossing point of the horizontal and the falling line, where the velocity is $v_0 = 90\,\text{km/h}$

Find

the moment of stopping $t_\text{s}$ and the braking distance $\Delta x$ from the beginning of the braking

Principle

The train stops when the braking line crosses the time axis; because the measurement ended at $40\,\text{s}$, the line has to be extended outside the measured range. The displacement is the area between the $(t,v)$ graph and the time axis, here a right-angled triangle (chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion).

Reading the graph

The horizontal and the falling line cross at about $t_0 = 10\,\text{s}$, and the extended braking line crosses the time axis at about $t_\text{s} = 48.5\,\text{s}$.

Formula

$\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}\,v_0\,(t_\text{s} - t_0)$

Conversions

$90\,\text{km/h}$$= \dfrac{90 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= 25\,\text{m/s}$

Substitution

$\Delta x$$= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 25\,\text{m/s} \cdot (48.5\,\text{s} - 10\,\text{s})$
$= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 25\,\text{m/s} \cdot 38.5\,\text{s}$
$= 481\,\text{m}$

Answer

The train stops at about $49\,\text{s}$, and the braking distance is about $480\,\text{m}$. Two significant figures, because the crossing points are read from the graph to within about half a second; results of $450$–$520\,\text{m}$ are accepted.

Reasonableness

With the acceleration of part b, braking from $25\,\text{m/s}$ lasts $25\,\text{m/s} : 0.645\,\text{m/s}^2 \approx 39\,\text{s}$, like the $38.5\,\text{s}$ read from the graph. The average velocity during the braking is $12.5\,\text{m/s}$, and $12.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 38.5\,\text{s} \approx 480\,\text{m}$. A braking distance of about half a kilometre suits a train that stops at a station with ordinary braking.

d)

Find

how reliable the conclusion is that the train stopped exactly at the distance of part c

Principle

A model is reliable only in the range where it has been measured; extending it outside the measured range, extrapolation, assumes that the motion continues in the same way. The precision of the result also depends on how well the slope is known (chapters Graphing and fitting a straight line and Measuring and the reliability of a measurement).

Model answer

The conclusion is good in its order of magnitude but not exact. The recording ended at $40\,\text{s}$, when the velocity was still about $20\,\text{km/h}$, so the moment of stopping $48.5\,\text{s}$ and the distance of the last $9\,\text{s}$ or so rest on the assumption that the train braked with the same acceleration until the end. In reality a driver often eases the braking just before stopping, and then the train goes a little further. In addition, the uncertainty of the slope carries over to the result: the steepest and the gentlest line that fit the points give a range of about a second for the moment of stopping and about $\pm 20\,\text{m}$ for the distance. The app's velocity uncertainty $\pm 1\,\text{km/h}$ is small compared with the change in velocity of $70\,\text{km/h}$, so it is not the largest source of uncertainty. The result should be given as “about $480\,\text{m}$, with an uncertainty of a few tens of metres”, not as “exactly”.

Common mistakes

  • All the points are joined into a broken line, or one straight line is fitted to all eleven points, so that the points of the uniform stage tilt the slope.
  • The slope is calculated from two adjacent measured points or from the table values instead of the line.
  • The acceleration is left in kilometres per hour per second, or in the conversion the number is multiplied by $3.6$ instead of divided.
  • The braking distance is calculated as the rectangle $25\,\text{m/s} \cdot 38.5\,\text{s}$ instead of the triangle, or the distance is calculated from $0$.

Also accepted

  • The slope of part b from a spreadsheet fit to the braking points: $-2.31\,\text{km/h}$ per second, that is, $-0.642\,\text{m/s}^2$.
  • The distance of part c calculated from the acceleration of part b: $t = v_0 : |a|$ and $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}\,v_0\,t$.

2. The photoelectric effect and the Planck constant 20 points

Example data.

In a physics lab a photocell whose cathode is potassium is studied. When the cathode is lit, electrons leave it and travel through a vacuum to the anode, and a photocurrent flows in the circuit. A voltage that slows the electrons down is connected between the anode and the cathode: the anode is more negative than the cathode. The voltage is increased until the photocurrent just stops. This voltage is called the stopping voltage $U$. The cathode was lit in turn with different wavelengths using an LED light source and filters.

$\lambda\ (\text{nm})$ $U\ (\text{V})$
$365$ $1.15$
$405$ $0.80$
$436$ $0.61$
$470$ $0.38$
$505$ $0.21$
$590$ no current
  • $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$, $c = 2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}$
  1. a) Present the stopping voltage as a function of the frequency of the light as a graph. 4 p
  2. b) Justify why a straight line fits the points, and fit a straight line to the points. Extend the line so that it crosses both axes. 5 p
  3. c) Determine the Planck constant from the graph. Compare the result with the value $6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$. 6 p
  4. d) Determine from the graph the work function of potassium in electronvolts and the threshold frequency. Explain why no photocurrent arises at the wavelength $590\,\text{nm}$, and whether the situation would change if there were ten times more $590\,\text{nm}$ light. 5 p

Skills from the chapters: The photoelectric effect and the photon, Potential energy and potential in an electric field, Graphing and fitting a straight line

Show the model answer and marking Open only when your own solution is ready.

Model answer

a)

Find

the stopping voltage $U$ as a function of the frequency $f$ of the light as a graph

Graph

The frequency is calculated from the wavelength, $f = \tfrac{c}{\lambda}$: for example $\tfrac{2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{365 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}} = 8.214 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$. The frequencies are $8.214$, $7.402$, $6.876$, $6.379$ and $5.937$ times $10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$ for the wavelengths $365$–$505\,\text{nm}$. On the horizontal axis is $f\ (10^{14}\,\text{Hz})$ and on the vertical axis $U\ (\text{V})$. No point is plotted for the wavelength $590\,\text{nm}$, because no photocurrent arises at all, so there is no stopping voltage. The points are not joined into a broken line.

Model answer

The points rise steadily as the frequency increases and lie roughly on the same straight line. A larger frequency gives a larger stopping voltage, that is the electrons leave with a larger kinetic energy.

b)

Find

a justification that a straight line fits the points, the fitted line and its extension to the axes

Graph

One photon gives all its energy $hf$ to one electron. Part of the energy is used for the work function $W$, and the fastest electron is left with the kinetic energy $E_\text{k,max} = hf - W$ (chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon).

When the anode is more negative than the cathode, the electric field brakes the electron, and its kinetic energy turns into electric potential energy. The current stops when even the fastest electron stops just before the anode: $eU = E_\text{k,max}$ (chapter Potential energy and potential in an electric field).

Combining these gives $eU = hf - W$, that is $U = \tfrac{h}{e}\,f - \tfrac{W}{e}$, so $U$ is a linear function of the frequency with slope $\tfrac{h}{e}$ and constant term $-\tfrac{W}{e}$. The line is fitted between the points and extended as a dashed line to the vertical axis, because the constant term is needed to determine the work function (chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line).

Model answer

According to the model $U = \tfrac{h}{e}\,f - \tfrac{W}{e}$, the points should lie on a straight line, and indeed they lie on the same line to within about $0.01\,\text{V}$. The fitted line crosses the vertical axis at about $-2.24\,\text{V}$ and the horizontal axis at about $5.43 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$. The extension of the line to negative voltages is only a mathematical aid: at frequencies below the threshold frequency no electrons leave at all.

c)

Given

the fitted line of part b, $e = 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C}$

Find

the Planck constant $h$ and its comparison with the value $6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$

Principle

According to the model $U = \tfrac{h}{e}\,f - \tfrac{W}{e}$, the slope of the line is $k = \tfrac{h}{e}$. The slope is read from two points of the fitted line far apart, not from the measurement points (chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line).

Reading the graph

The points $(0$; $-2.24\,\text{V})$ and $(8.0 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$; $1.06\,\text{V})$ are read from the line.

Formula

$k = \dfrac{\Delta U}{\Delta f}$ and $k = \dfrac{h}{e}$, so $h = ek$

Conversions

$1\,\text{C} \cdot 1\,\text{V} \cdot 1\,\text{s} = 1\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$, because $1\,\text{V} = 1\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{C}}$ and $\tfrac{1}{\text{Hz}} = 1\,\text{s}$.

Substitution

$k$$= \dfrac{1.06\,\text{V} - (-2.24\,\text{V})}{8.0 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz} - 0}$
$= \dfrac{3.30\,\text{V}}{8.0 \cdot 10^{14}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}}$
$= 4.125 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$

so $$ \begin{aligned} h &= 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 4.125 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s} \\ &= 6.608 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \end{aligned} $$$$ \begin{aligned} h &= 1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\text{C} \cdot 4.125 \\ &\cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s} \\ &= 6.608 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \end{aligned} $$

Answer

$h \approx 6.61 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$

Three significant figures, because the voltages are read from the graph to about a hundredth of a volt. The result is $0.3\,\%$ smaller than the table value $6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$.

Reasonableness

The difference is much smaller than the reading uncertainty, about $1\,\%$, so the measurement supports the model. The slope does not depend on the material of the cathode, so any metal would give the same $h$.

d)

Given

the line of part b, which crosses the vertical axis at $b = -2.24\,\text{V}$, slope $k = 4.125 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$ (part c), $\lambda = 590\,\text{nm}$

Find

the work function $W$ in electronvolts, the threshold frequency $f_0$ and an explanation of the result at the wavelength $590\,\text{nm}$

Principle

The constant term of the line is $-\tfrac{W}{e}$, so the work function in electronvolts is the absolute value of the constant term. At the threshold frequency the photon energy is just enough for the work function and $U = 0$, so it is where the line crosses the horizontal axis. The intensity of the light changes only the number of photons, not the energy of one photon (chapter The photoelectric effect and the photon).

Reading the graph

The line crosses the vertical axis at $-2.24\,\text{V}$ and the horizontal axis at about $5.43 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$.

Formula

$b = -\dfrac{W}{e}$, so $W = -e\,b$; $hf_0 = W$, so $f_0 = \dfrac{W}{h} = -\dfrac{b}{k}$, because $k = \dfrac{h}{e}$; $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$ and $E = hf$

Conversions

$e \cdot 1\,\text{V} = 1\,\text{eV}$, so $W = 2.24\,\text{eV}$ directly. The wavelength $590\,\text{nm} = 590 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}$, and the energy is converted into electronvolts by dividing by $1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{eV}}$.

Substitution

$W$$= -e \cdot (-2.24\,\text{V})$
$= 2.24\,\text{eV}$
$f_0$$= -\dfrac{-2.24\,\text{V}}{4.125 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}}$
$= 5.43 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$

At the wavelength $590\,\text{nm}$:

$f$$= \dfrac{2.998 \cdot 10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{590 \cdot 10^{-9}\,\text{m}}$
$= 5.08 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$
$E$$= \dfrac{6.626 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s} \cdot 5.08 \cdot 10^{14}\,\tfrac{1}{\text{s}}}{1.602 \cdot 10^{-19}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{eV}}}$
$= 2.10\,\text{eV}$

Answer

The work function of potassium is $W \approx 2.24\,\text{eV}$ and the threshold frequency $f_0 \approx 5.43 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$, three significant figures by the reading accuracy of the graph. The frequency $5.08 \cdot 10^{14}\,\text{Hz}$ of light of wavelength $590\,\text{nm}$ is below the threshold frequency, that is the energy $2.10\,\text{eV}$ of one photon is not enough to free an electron. Ten times as much light means ten times as many photons of the same kind. One photon gives its energy to one electron, so still no electrons leave, and no photocurrent arises.

Reasonableness

The threshold frequency corresponds to the wavelength $\tfrac{c}{f_0} \approx 552\,\text{nm}$, green light, so yellow $590\,\text{nm}$ light is on the wrong side of the limit and all the measured violet and blue wavelengths on the right side, as in the table. The work functions of the alkali metals are about $2\,\text{eV}$, so the result is credible.

Common mistakes

  • In part a, the wavelength is plotted on the horizontal axis, so the points are not on a straight line, or the points are joined into a broken line.
  • In part a, a point $U = 0$ is plotted for the wavelength $590\,\text{nm}$, although no current arises at all.
  • In part b, the line is forced through the origin, so the work function cannot be determined.
  • In part c, the slope is read from two measurement points and not from the fitted line, or $10^{14}$ is forgotten, which gives $h \approx 6.6 \cdot 10^{-20}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$.
  • In part d, the work function is given as negative or in joules, although electronvolts are asked for.
  • In part d, it is claimed that brighter light frees electrons because there is more energy.

Also accepted

  • In part c, a fit with a calculator or spreadsheet: $k = 4.13 \cdot 10^{-15}\,\text{V}\,\text{s}$, $h = 6.61 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J}\,\text{s}$ and constant term $-2.24\,\text{V}$.
  • In part d, the threshold frequency from the equation of the line $f_0 = \tfrac{W}{h}$ with the value of part c, or directly from where the line crosses the horizontal axis.
