Mini exam 60 min: data task (1)

Time 60 min, at most 20 points. Hints, feedback and model answers open when you end the exam.

  • Choose 1 of the 2 tasks before you answer.

1. Vaporisation of boiling water 20 points

Example data.

A group determined the specific latent heat of vaporisation of water. An open steel container of water stood on a balance, and in the water there was an immersion heater whose power could be adjusted. When the water was boiling steadily, the group read the change $\Delta m$ in the reading of the balance over five minutes ($300\,\text{s}$). The measurement was repeated at five powers $P$.

$P\ (\text{W})$ $\Delta m\ (\text{g})$
$200$ $17.9$
$400$ $43.1$
$600$ $70.8$
$800$ $96.4$
$1000$ $123.8$

A member of the group calculated the result from the $600\,\text{W}$ measurement alone: $r = \dfrac{P\,t}{\Delta m} \approx 2.54\,\text{MJ/kg}$.

  1. a) Draw a graph of the mass of water vaporised as a function of the power of the heater, and fit a suitable model to the points. 5 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    Which quantity does the group set and which is the measured result? Which one belongs on the horizontal axis?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    Plot the five points $(P,\ \Delta m)$ and fit a line that runs between the points. Look at whether it passes through the origin.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Look at part c: what must you be able to read from the graph? Choose a scale on the axes where that is possible, and think what you must do to the line.

  2. b) Determine the specific latent heat of vaporisation of water with the help of the graph. 6 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    The heater supplies energy that the water takes as it vaporises. Which feature of the graph tells how much more water vaporises when the power increases?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    The energy needed for vaporisation is $Q = r\,\Delta m$. Of the heater's energy $P\,t$, the part $P_\text{loss}\,t$ is conducted to the room: $(P - P_\text{loss})\,t = r\,\Delta m$. Work out what the slope of the line tells.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Read the slope from two points of the line on the graph and convert its unit from grams to kilograms. Then solve $r$ with the slope and the time $t = 300\,\text{s}$.

  3. c) The line does not pass through the origin. Determine from the graph at what power the water would not vaporise at all, and interpret the result. 4 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    Which point of the graph tells at what power the vaporised mass is zero? Where does the power of the heater go then?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    By conservation of energy $(P - P_\text{loss})\,t = r\,\Delta m$, where $P_\text{loss}$ is the power that does not vaporise water. What does the condition $\Delta m = 0$ mean for this equation?

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Read the point where the extended line meets the horizontal axis. You can also solve it from the equation of the line with $\Delta m = 0$.

  4. d) Assess why the value $2.54\,\text{MJ/kg}$ calculated from one measurement differs from the result of the graph, and which result is more reliable. 5 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    What does the calculation from one measurement, $r = \dfrac{P\,t}{\Delta m}$, assume about the power of the heater? Is the assumption true in a real experiment?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    Compare the formula for one measurement with the equation of the graph's model, $(P - P_\text{loss})\,t = r\,\Delta m$. What is the difference, and in which direction does it distort the result?

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Calculate the difference as a percentage: $\dfrac{2.54\,\text{MJ/kg} - r_\text{graph}}{r_\text{graph}}$. Then consider what a loss that stays constant does to the line.

Skills from the chapters: Changes of state, Graphing and fitting a straight line, Heat transfer, energy balance and climate change

Model answer

a)

Find

a graph of the mass of water vaporised $\Delta m$ as a function of the power $P$ of the heater and a model fitted to it

Graph

The power $P\ (\text{W})$ is on the horizontal axis and the mass $\Delta m\ (\text{g})$ on the vertical axis. The scales start from zero, so that the point where the line crosses the horizontal axis can be seen for part c.

The circled points in the graph are the two points of the line from which the slope of part b is read. The orange point is where the line crosses the horizontal axis (part c).

Model answer

The points lie almost on a straight line: the mass vaporised grows steadily with the power. One rising straight line passing between the points is fitted to them. The line is not forced through the origin, because no physical reason requires it: part of the power can go into something other than vaporisation. The line is extended as a dashed line to the horizontal axis, and it crosses the axis clearly to the right of zero.

b)

Given

the line of part a and the points read from it; measuring time $t = 300\,\text{s}$

Find

the specific latent heat of vaporisation of water $r$ in $\text{MJ/kg}$

Principle

The water boils all the time, so its temperature does not rise, and the part of the heater's power that is not conducted to the surroundings goes into vaporisation. Energy is conserved: $(P - P_\text{loss})\,t = r\,\Delta m$, where $P_\text{loss}$ is the loss power. From this $\Delta m = \dfrac{t}{r}\,P - \dfrac{t}{r}\,P_\text{loss}$, so the slope of the line is $\dfrac{t}{r}$ (chapters Changes of state and Graphing and fitting a straight line).

Reading the graph

Two points of the line far apart are read from the line, not measured points: $(100\,\text{W}$; $4.0\,\text{g})$ and $(1100\,\text{W}$; $137.0\,\text{g})$.

Formula

$k$$= \dfrac{\Delta(\Delta m)}{\Delta P}$
$= \dfrac{t}{r}$

so $r = \dfrac{t}{k}$

Conversions

The slope from grams to kilograms: $1\,\text{g} = 10^{-3}\,\text{kg}$. The unit

$\tfrac{\text{s}}{\text{kg/W}}$$= \tfrac{\text{W}\cdot\text{s}}{\text{kg}}$
$= \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$

and finally $1\,\text{MJ} = 10^{6}\,\text{J}$.

Substitution

$k$$= \dfrac{137.0\,\text{g} - 4.0\,\text{g}}{1100\,\text{W} - 100\,\text{W}}$
$= \dfrac{133.0\,\text{g}}{1000\,\text{W}}$
$= 0.133\,\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{W}}$
$r$$= \dfrac{300\,\text{s}}{0.133 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\tfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{W}}}$
$= 2.26 \cdot 10^{6}\,\tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{kg}}$
$= 2.26\,\text{MJ/kg}$

Answer

$r \approx 2.26\,\text{MJ/kg}$

Three significant figures is the upper limit, because the slope of a line fitted by eye is accurate to about $\pm 2\,\%$; $2.3\,\text{MJ/kg}$ is an equally good answer.

Reasonableness

The table value is $2.26\,\text{MJ/kg}$, and the least-squares line also gives $2.26\,\text{MJ/kg}$. Check with one point: at $600\,\text{W}$ about $530\,\text{W}$ is left for vaporisation, and

$530\,\text{W} \cdot 300\,\text{s} : 2.26\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$$\approx 70\,\text{g}$
$530\,\text{W} \cdot 300\,\text{s} : 2.26\,\tfrac{\text{MJ}}{\text{kg}}$
$\approx 70\,\text{g}$

as in the table.

c)

Given

the line of part a, which crosses the horizontal axis; the slope $k = 0.133\,\text{g/W}$ of part b and the point of the line $(P_1$; $\Delta m_1) = (100\,\text{W}$; $4.0\,\text{g})$

Find

the power $P_\text{loss}$ at which, according to the line, the water would not vaporise, and its interpretation

Principle

Where the line crosses the horizontal axis, $\Delta m = 0$. According to the energy balance $(P - P_\text{loss})\,t = r\,\Delta m$ this happens when $P = P_\text{loss}$: the whole power of the heater is then conducted through the walls of the container and the water surface to the room. Boiling water is always at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, so the temperature difference to the room, and therefore the loss power, stay the same at all powers (chapter Heat transfer, energy balance and climate change).

Reading the graph

The line extended as a dashed line crosses the horizontal axis at about $70\,\text{W}$.

Formula

$0 = k\,(P_\text{loss} - P_1) + \Delta m_1$, so

$P_\text{loss}$$= P_1 - \dfrac{\Delta m_1}{k}$

Conversions

None needed: the slope and the mass are in grams and the power in watts.

Substitution

$P_\text{loss}$$= 100\,\text{W} - \dfrac{4.0\,\text{g}}{0.133\,\tfrac{\text{g}}{\text{W}}}$
$= 100\,\text{W} - 30\,\text{W}$
$= 70\,\text{W}$

Answer

$P_\text{loss} \approx 70\,\text{W}$

That is, about $70\,\text{W}$ of the heater's power is conducted to the room and does not vaporise water. One or two significant figures, because the intercept is found by extending the line outside the measured range, and a small change in the slope moves it by tens of watts.

Reasonableness

The least-squares line crosses the axis at $69\,\text{W}$. The loss power is a few per cent of the largest power but more than a third of the smallest, which suits conduction and convection from an open steel container.

d)

Find

why the result $2.54\,\text{MJ/kg}$ from one measurement is too large and which result is more reliable

Principle

Energy is conserved: the energy $P\,t$ of the heater becomes internal energy of the water and the container; part of it vaporises the water and part is transferred as heat to the room, $P\,t = r\,\Delta m + P_\text{loss}\,t$ (chapter Changes of state).

Model answer

In the calculation $r = \dfrac{P\,t}{\Delta m}$ from one measurement, all the energy of the heater is assumed to have gone into vaporisation. In reality about $70\,\text{W}$ is conducted to the room, so at the power $600\,\text{W}$ only about $530\,\text{W}$ is left for vaporisation. The calculation therefore has too much energy per unit of mass, and the result is about $\dfrac{P}{P - P_\text{h}} = 600 : 530 \approx 1.13$ times, that is about $13\,\%$, too large. The result from the graph is more reliable. A constant loss power shifts the line sideways but does not change its slope, so the $r$ calculated from the slope does not include the loss. In addition, five measurements and a line fitted between them reduce the effect of the random error of a single reading. The method assumes that the loss power is the same at all powers. This is justified, because boiling water is always at $100\,{}^\circ\text{C}$, and the points lying on a straight line support the assumption.

Common mistakes

  • The line is forced through the origin, so the slope and $r$ are distorted and the loss power is not seen.
  • The slope is calculated from two neighbouring measured points and not from the fitted line.
  • In part b grams are not converted to kilograms, so the result is a thousand times too small.
  • In part b the slope itself is taken as $r$ instead of $\dfrac{t}{k}$.
  • In part d only "measurement error" is said, without saying which energy has been calculated wrongly and in which direction it changes the result.

Also accepted

  • A slope of $0.128$…$0.138\,\text{g/W}$ when the line has been fitted by eye between the points, and the corresponding results: $2.2$–$2.3\,\text{MJ/kg}$ in part b and $50$–$90\,\text{W}$ in part c.
  • In part b the mass produced can also be calculated per second: $\dfrac{\Delta m}{t}$ as a function of power, and then the slope is $\dfrac{1}{r}$.

2. Torch batteries under load 20 points

Example data.

Students investigated the battery holder of a torch, which contains three new AA batteries in series. A variable resistor and an ammeter were connected in series to the terminals of the holder, and a voltmeter across the terminals of the holder. The variable resistor was adjusted, and at each setting the current $I$ and the terminal voltage $U$ were read.

$I\ (\text{A})$ $U\ (\text{V})$
$0.10$ $4.56$
$0.25$ $4.43$
$0.40$ $4.34$
$0.60$ $4.18$
$0.80$ $4.06$
$1.00$ $3.89$
  1. a) Present the terminal voltage as a function of the current as a graph and fit a model to the points. Justify the choice of model. 5 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    Which quantity was adjusted in the measurement, and which one followed from it? Which axes should the graph have?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    The terminal voltage of a battery is the emf minus the voltage drop $R_\text{s} I$ across the internal resistance. Think about how this drop depends on the current and what kind of graph it produces.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Plot the points and think about what kind of line the model of hint 2 would give. How far should the line be extended, and why?

  2. b) Determine the emf and the internal resistance of the group of batteries from the graph. Compare the results with the typical values of a new AA battery, which are an emf of $1.5$–$1.6\,\text{V}$ and an internal resistance of $0.1$–$0.3\,\Omega$. 6 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    When is the terminal voltage equal to the emf? What does the intercept on the vertical axis tell you, and what does the slope of the line tell you?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    The equation $U = E - R_\text{s} I$ is the equation of a line. Its intercept on the vertical axis is $E$ and its slope is $-R_\text{s}$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Read two points from the line that are far apart and calculate the slope $\dfrac{\Delta U}{\Delta I}$. The internal resistance is its negative. For the comparison: there are three batteries in series.

  3. c) How large a current would flow if the terminals of the holder were short-circuited? Why was this current not measured, and how reliable is the result? 4 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    What does a short circuit mean? How large is the terminal voltage when the terminals are joined directly with a wire?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    In a short circuit the terminal voltage is zero: $0 = E - R_\text{s} I_\text{k}$. On the graph this is the point where the line crosses the horizontal axis.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Solve the short-circuit current $I_\text{k}$ and substitute the values from part b. Then think about how far the result is from the measured currents, and what would happen to the batteries if the current were measured.

  4. d) The resistance of the torch bulb is $3.6\,\Omega$ when it is lit. What power is transferred to the bulb, and what fraction of the source power $EI$ of the batteries is it? 5 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    The bulb and the internal resistance of the batteries are in the same loop. What is the total resistance of the circuit then?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    The current is $I = \dfrac{E}{R + R_\text{s}}$, the power of the bulb is $P = RI^2$ and the source power of the batteries is $EI$. The fraction is the power of the bulb divided by the source power.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    First calculate the current $I$ with the values of part b. Then calculate the power of the bulb and the source power separately and divide them.

Model answer

a)

Find

the terminal voltage as a function of the current as a graph, a fitted model and the justification of the model

Graph

$I\ (\text{A})$ is on the horizontal axis and $U\ (\text{V})$ on the vertical axis. The voltages are between $3.89$ and $4.56\,\text{V}$, so the vertical axis is broken so that the small changes show; the break is marked on the axis. The horizontal axis starts at zero, so that the line can be extended to the vertical axis. The points are not joined into a broken line; instead one straight line is fitted between them.

Model answer

The group of batteries is described by a model in which a source with the emf $E$ is in series with the internal resistance $R_\text{s}$. When the current $I$ is drawn from the group, the terminal voltage is smaller by the voltage drop across the internal resistance, $U = E - R_\text{s} I$.

The voltage drop is proportional to the current when $E$ and $R_\text{s}$ are constant, so the graph of the model is a falling straight line. The points lie well on a straight line, and their deviations from the line are about $0.01\,\text{V}$, of the order of the precision of the readings. So the model fits the data.

b)

Given

the fitted line in the graph; the points read from the line $(0\,\text{A}$; $4.63\,\text{V})$ and $(1.20\,\text{A}$; $3.75\,\text{V})$; the typical values of a new AA battery $1.5$–$1.6\,\text{V}$ and $0.1$–$0.3\,\Omega$

Find

the emf $E$ and the internal resistance $R_\text{s}$ of the group of batteries and a comparison with the values of one battery

Principle

According to the model $U = E - R_\text{s} I$ the line crosses the vertical axis at $U = E$, because without a current there is no voltage drop across the internal resistance, and the slope of the line is $-R_\text{s}$ (chapter Electromotive force and internal resistance). The slope is read from two points of the fitted line far apart, not from the measured points (chapter Graphing and fitting a straight line). The emfs and the internal resistances of batteries connected in series add up.

Reading the graph

The line crosses the vertical axis at $4.63\,\text{V}$. At $I = 1.20\,\text{A}$ the value of the line is $3.75\,\text{V}$.

Formula

$E$$= U(0)$
$R_\text{s}$$= -\dfrac{\Delta U}{\Delta I}$

where $U(0)$ is the value of the line at zero current and $\Delta U$ and $\Delta I$ are the differences between the points read from the line; the values of three batteries in series add up, so per battery $E_1 = \dfrac{E}{3}$ and $R_{\text{s}1} = \dfrac{R_\text{s}}{3}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$E$$= 4.63\,\text{V}$
$R_\text{s}$$= -\dfrac{3.75\,\text{V} - 4.63\,\text{V}}{1.20\,\text{A} - 0\,\text{A}}$
$= \dfrac{0.88\,\text{V}}{1.20\,\text{A}}$
$= 0.733\,\Omega$

per battery

$\dfrac{4.63\,\text{V}}{3}$$= 1.54\,\text{V}$
$\dfrac{0.733\,\Omega}{3}$$= 0.244\,\Omega$

Answer

The emf is $E \approx 4.63\,\text{V}$ and the internal resistance $R_\text{s} \approx 0.73\,\Omega$. The internal resistance is given to two significant figures, because the change in voltage $0.88\,\text{V}$ is read from the graph to about a hundredth.

Reasonableness

Per battery, $1.54\,\text{V}$ and $0.24\,\Omega$ are typical values of a new AA battery. The $4.56\,\text{V}$ measured at the smallest current is a little below the emf, as it should be.

c)

Given

$E \approx 4.63\,\text{V}$ and $R_\text{s} \approx 0.733\,\Omega$ (part b), largest measured current $1.00\,\text{A}$

Find

the short-circuit current $I_\text{k}$, why it was not measured and how reliable the result is

Principle

In a short circuit the resistance of the external circuit is zero, so the current is limited only by the internal resistance (chapter Electromotive force and internal resistance). In the graph the short-circuit current is the intersection of the line with the horizontal axis, where the terminal voltage is zero.

Formula

$I_\text{k} = \dfrac{E}{R_\text{s}}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$I_\text{k}$$= \dfrac{4.63\,\text{V}}{0.733\,\Omega}$
$= 6.31\,\text{A}$

Answer

The short-circuit current would be about $I_\text{k} \approx 6.3\,\text{A}$, two significant figures like the internal resistance. It was not measured, because in a short circuit the batteries would heat up quickly and could be damaged. The result is only an estimate: it is an extrapolation to more than six times the current, outside the measuring range, and it assumes that the internal resistance stays the same at large currents too.

Reasonableness

The line extended in the graph crosses the axis at the same place, about $6.3\,\text{A}$. The short-circuit current of a new AA battery is a few amperes, so the result is credible. In reality the chemistry of the batteries cannot keep up with a large current, and the voltage falls more than the model predicts, so the real current would probably be smaller.

d)

Given

resistance of the bulb $R = 3.6\,\Omega$, $E \approx 4.63\,\text{V}$ and $R_\text{s} \approx 0.733\,\Omega$ (part b)

Find

the power $P$ transferred to the bulb and its fraction of the source power $EI$

Principle

The bulb and the internal resistance are in series in the same loop, so the current is set by the sum of their resistances (chapter Electromotive force and internal resistance). The power of the bulb is obtained from its resistance and current with Joule's law, and the source produces electrical energy at the power $EI$ (chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor).

Formula

$I = \dfrac{E}{R + R_\text{s}}$, $P = RI^2$ and the fraction $= \dfrac{P}{EI} = \dfrac{R}{R + R_\text{s}}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$I$$= \dfrac{4.63\,\text{V}}{3.6\,\Omega + 0.733\,\Omega}$
$= 1.069\,\text{A}$
$P$$= 3.6\,\Omega \cdot (1.069\,\text{A})^2$
$= 4.11\,\text{W}$
$EI$$= 4.63\,\text{V} \cdot 1.069\,\text{A}$
$= 4.95\,\text{W}$
$\dfrac{4.11\,\text{W}}{4.95\,\text{W}}$$= 0.831$

Answer

The power transferred to the bulb is $P \approx 4.1\,\text{W}$, which is about $83\,\%$ of the source power. The precision is two significant figures like the resistance of the bulb $3.6\,\Omega$.

Reasonableness

The internal resistance takes $$ \begin{aligned} 0.733\,\Omega \cdot (1.069\,\text{A})^2 &\approx 0.84\,\text{W}\text{,} \end{aligned} $$$$ \begin{aligned} &0.733\,\Omega \cdot (1.069\,\text{A})^2 \\ &\approx 0.84\,\text{W}\text{,} \end{aligned} $$ and $$ 4.11\,\text{W} + 0.84\,\text{W} = 4.95\,\text{W}\text{,}$$ as conservation of energy requires. The fraction is also

$\tfrac{R}{R + R_\text{s}}$$= \tfrac{3.6}{4.33}$
$\approx 0.83$

The current $1.07\,\text{A}$ is slightly above the measuring range, so the model probably holds well for it.

Common mistakes

  • In part a the points are joined into a broken line, or the vertical axis is broken without marking the break.
  • In part b the terminal voltage at the smallest current, $4.56\,\text{V}$, is given as the emf, or the slope is calculated from two measured points instead of the line.
  • In part b the internal resistance is calculated as the ratio $\tfrac{U}{I}$ of one measurement, which is the resistance of the load resistor.
  • In part c the short-circuit current is calculated from the terminal voltage, or the result is considered as reliable as the measured values.
  • In part d the internal resistance is forgotten, so that $I = \tfrac{4.63\,\text{V}}{3.6\,\Omega} = 1.29\,\text{A}$ and $P = 5.95\,\text{W}$.

Also accepted

  • In part b the equation of the fitted line with a calculator: $U = 4.625\,\text{V} - 0.727\,\Omega \cdot I$, so $E \approx 4.63\,\text{V}$ and $R_\text{s} \approx 0.73\,\Omega$.
  • In part d the power from the terminal voltage: $U = E - R_\text{s} I = 3.85\,\text{V}$ and $P = UI \approx 4.1\,\text{W}$.
