Mini exam 60 min: Part II (2)

Time 60 min, at most 21 points. Hints, feedback and model answers open when you end the exam.

  • Choose 1 of the 2 tasks before you answer.

1. Find the mistake: the radiator 2 points

A student calculated how much energy a $2.0\,\text{kW}$ electric radiator uses in $30\,\text{min}$: “$E = P\,t = 2.0\,\text{kW} \cdot 30\,\text{min} = 60\,\text{kWh}$.” What is wrong with the solution?

  • Correct. The product of power and time is in kilowatt-hours only when the time is in hours: $30\,\text{min} = 0.50\,\text{h}$, so $$ \begin{aligned} E &= 2.0\,\text{kW} \cdot 0.50\,\text{h} \\ &= 1.0\,\text{kWh}\text{.} \end{aligned} $$

  • Power is energy divided by time, $P = \Delta E : \Delta t$, so energy is power multiplied by time. The formula is correct; the mistake is in the unit: the minutes must be converted to hours.

  • The kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, and energy is what is asked here. The mistake is in the time: $30\,\text{min} = 0.50\,\text{h}$, so the correct energy is $1.0\,\text{kWh}$.

2. Loudspeaker at an open-air concert 2 points

At an open-air concert an intensity level of $104\,\text{dB}$ is measured $20\,\text{m}$ from the loudspeaker tower. Assume that the tower is a point source that radiates evenly in all directions and that the sound is neither reflected nor absorbed in the air.

How far from the tower has the level dropped to $85\,\text{dB}$?

m
Hint 1: a guiding question

How many times greater is the intensity $20\,\text{m}$ away than at the point asked about?

Hint 2: what is the connection

$L_1 - L_2 = 10\,\text{dB} \cdot \lg \tfrac{I_1}{I_2}$, so $\tfrac{I_1}{I_2} = 10^{1.9}$.

Hint 3: an intermediate step

For a point source $I = \tfrac{P}{4\pi r^2}$, so $\tfrac{I_1}{I_2} = \tfrac{r_2^2}{r_1^2}$ and $r_2 = r_1\sqrt{\tfrac{I_1}{I_2}}$.

Show the solution

Given

distance $r_1 = 20\,\text{m}$, the level there $L_1 = 104\,\text{dB}$, at the point asked about $L_2 = 85\,\text{dB}$; a point source without reflections or absorption; the factor of the decibel scale $\beta = 10\,\text{dB}$ (by definition)

Find

the distance $r_2$ in $\text{m}$

Principle

The difference of the levels depends only on the ratio of the intensities, because the threshold of hearing $I_0$ cancels out (chapter Intensity and the decibel scale). The power of a point source spreads over the surface of a sphere $4\pi r^2$, so the intensity is inversely proportional to the square of the distance.

Formula

with the decibel factor $\beta$,

$L_1 - L_2$$= \beta \cdot \lg \dfrac{I_1}{I_2}$
$\dfrac{I_1}{I_2}$$= \dfrac{r_2^2}{r_1^2}$

so $r_2 = r_1 \sqrt{10^{\,(L_1 - L_2)/\beta}}$

Conversions

The quantities are already in the same units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$\dfrac{L_1 - L_2}{10\,\text{dB}}$$= \dfrac{19\,\text{dB}}{10\,\text{dB}}$
$= 1.9$

so

$\dfrac{I_1}{I_2}$$= 10^{1.9}$
$= 79.43$
$r_2$$= 20\,\text{m} \cdot \sqrt{79.43}$
$= 20\,\text{m} \cdot 8.913$
$= 178.3\,\text{m}$

Answer

$r_2 \approx 180\,\text{m}$

Two significant figures like the distance $20\,\text{m}$.

Reasonableness

Doubling the distance lowers the level by about $6\,\text{dB}$. A drop of $19\,\text{dB}$ corresponds to about three doublings, $2^3 \cdot 20\,\text{m} = 160\,\text{m}$, and a little more, so $180\,\text{m}$ is of the right order of magnitude. In reality the ground and buildings reflect sound and the air absorbs it, so the result is only an estimate.

3. Read the graph: binding energy per nucleon 2 points

The graph shows the binding energy per nucleon of nuclei as a function of the mass number. Which claim is correct?

  • In the graph $\tfrac{E_\text{B}}{A}$ decreases from about $8.5\,\text{MeV}$ to about $7.6\,\text{MeV}$, so the product would be less tightly bound, and the reaction would need energy. Review in the chapter Mass defect and nuclear binding energy.

  • Correct. The binding energy per nucleon increases by about $0.9\,\text{MeV}$, so about $240 \cdot 0.9\,\text{MeV} \approx 220\,\text{MeV}$ is released, that is about $200\,\text{MeV}$. Review if needed in the chapter Fission, fusion and nuclear power.

  • The nuclei at the peak are more tightly bound than all the others. From them neither fusion nor fission can lead to a more tightly bound nucleus, so iron cannot be used as an energy source. Review in the chapter Mass defect and nuclear binding energy.

4. The acceleration lane 15 points

Example data.

A car enters the acceleration lane of a motorway at $54\,\text{km/h}$. The lane is $270\,\text{m}$ long, and on it the car accelerates uniformly so that its velocity at the end of the lane is $108\,\text{km/h}$. In the right-hand lane of the motorway a lorry is travelling at a constant velocity of $81\,\text{km/h}$. At the moment when the car is at the beginning of the acceleration lane, the lorry is $40\,\text{m}$ behind it. The lanes are parallel, so the positions can be measured along the same road.

  1. a) How long does the car's acceleration on the lane last? 4 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    The acceleration is not known yet. Which three quantities are known, and which relation ties them to the time without the acceleration?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    In uniformly accelerated motion the displacement is the area of the trapezium under the $(t,v)$ graph: $\Delta x = \dfrac{v_0 + v}{2}\,t$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Convert the velocities first: $54\,\text{km/h} = 15\,\text{m/s}$ and $108\,\text{km/h} = 30\,\text{m/s}$. Then solve the equation for the time $t$.

  2. b) How large is the car's acceleration? 3 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    The change in velocity and the time it took are now known. How is acceleration defined from them?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    Acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time: $a = \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$. In uniformly accelerated motion it is the same all the time.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    The change in velocity is $\Delta v = 30\,\text{m/s} - 15\,\text{m/s}$, and the time $\Delta t$ is the result of part a.

  3. c) Does the lorry draw level with the car during the acceleration? How small is the distance between the lorry and the car at its smallest? 5 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    At first the lorry is faster than the car, but the car accelerates. At which moment does the gap stop shrinking?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    Write both positions in the same coordinate system, with the origin at the start of the lane: $x_\text{A} = v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$ and $x_\text{R} = -40\,\text{m} + v_\text{R}\,t$.

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    The gap is smallest when the velocities are equal: $v_0 + a\,t = v_\text{R}$, where

    $v_\text{R}$$= 81\,\text{km/h}$
    $= 22.5\,\text{m/s}$

    Calculate the difference $x_\text{A} - x_\text{R}$ at that moment.

  4. d) Another driver accelerates uniformly on the same lane over the whole length of the lane, but only to $72\,\text{km/h}$. Explain with graphs why the lorry then catches up with this car, and why it is worth accelerating to the speed of the traffic on an acceleration lane. 3 p
    Hint 1: a guiding question

    Compare the car's greatest velocity with the lorry's velocity. Which vehicle is faster for the whole length of the lane?

    Hint 2: what is the connection

    Draw both $(t,v)$ graphs in the same coordinate system. The distance travelled is the area under the graph: whose area grows faster?

    Hint 3: an intermediate step

    Calculate how long the acceleration now takes: $\Delta x = \dfrac{v_0 + v}{2}\,t$, where $v = 72\,\text{km/h} = 20\,\text{m/s}$. Then calculate how far the lorry travels in the same time.

Skills from the chapters: Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion, Comparing the motions of two bodies, Uniform motion: speed, position and graphs

Show the model answer and marking Open only when your own solution is ready.

Model answer

a)

Given

length of the lane $\Delta x = 270\,\text{m}$, velocity at the beginning of the lane $v_0 = 54\,\text{km/h}$ and at the end $v = 108\,\text{km/h}$; the acceleration is uniform

Find

the duration of the acceleration $t$ in seconds

Principle

The acceleration is constant, so the $(t,v)$ graph is a straight line and the region under it is a trapezium. The displacement is the area of this region, so the average velocity is the mean of the initial and final velocities (chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion). The acceleration is not needed to calculate the time.

Formula

$\Delta x = \dfrac{v_0 + v}{2}\,t$, so $t = \dfrac{2\,\Delta x}{v_0 + v}$

Conversions

$$ \begin{aligned} 54\,\text{km/h} &= \dfrac{54 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} \\ &= 15\,\text{m/s} \end{aligned} $$$$ \begin{aligned} &54\,\text{km/h} \\ &= \dfrac{54 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}} \\ &= 15\,\text{m/s} \end{aligned} $$ and

$108\,\text{km/h}$$= \dfrac{108 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= 30\,\text{m/s}$

Substitution

$t$$= \dfrac{2 \cdot 270\,\text{m}}{15\,\text{m/s} + 30\,\text{m/s}}$
$= \dfrac{540\,\text{m}}{45\,\text{m/s}}$
$= 12.0\,\text{s}$

Answer

$t \approx 12\,\text{s}$

Two significant figures like the velocities $54\,\text{km/h}$ and $108\,\text{km/h}$.

Reasonableness

The average velocity is $22.5\,\text{m/s}$, and $22.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 12.0\,\text{s} = 270\,\text{m}$, the length of the lane. An acceleration of about ten seconds to motorway speed is usual for a car.

b)

Given

$v_0 = 15\,\text{m/s}$, $v = 30\,\text{m/s}$ and from part a $t = 12.0\,\text{s}$

Find

the acceleration $a$ in $\text{m/s}^2$

Principle

The acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time it took; in uniformly accelerated motion it is the same all the time and equal to the slope of the $(t,v)$ graph (chapter Acceleration and uniformly accelerated motion).

Formula

$a$$= \dfrac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}$
$= \dfrac{v - v_0}{t}$

Conversions

The velocities were converted to $\text{m/s}$ in part a, and the time is in seconds.

Substitution

$a$$= \dfrac{30\,\text{m/s} - 15\,\text{m/s}}{12.0\,\text{s}}$
$= \dfrac{15\,\text{m/s}}{12.0\,\text{s}}$
$= 1.25\,\text{m/s}^2$

Answer

$a \approx 1.3\,\text{m/s}^2$

Two significant figures like the velocities ($1.25\,\text{m/s}^2$ is also accepted).

Reasonableness

The velocity grows by $1.25\,\text{m/s} = 4.5\,\text{km/h}$ every second, so in twelve seconds it grows by $54\,\text{km/h}$, as in the data. A check with the equation of position: $$ \begin{aligned} &15\,\text{m/s} \cdot 12.0\,\text{s} + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1.25\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (12.0\,\text{s})^2 \\ &= 180\,\text{m} + 90\,\text{m} \\ &= 270\,\text{m}\text{.} \end{aligned} $$$$ \begin{aligned} &15\,\text{m/s} \cdot 12.0\,\text{s} \\ &+ \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1.25\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (12.0\,\text{s})^2 \\ &= 180\,\text{m} + 90\,\text{m} \\ &= 270\,\text{m}\text{.} \end{aligned} $$

c)

Given

origin at the beginning of the acceleration lane, positive direction the direction of driving; car: $x_{0\text{A}} = 0$, $v_0 = 15\,\text{m/s}$, $a = 1.25\,\text{m/s}^2$; lorry: $x_{0\text{L}} = -40\,\text{m}$, $v_\text{L} = 81\,\text{km/h}$ (constant)

Find

whether the lorry draws level with the car, and the smallest distance $d_\text{min}$

Principle

The positions are written in the same coordinate system (chapter Comparing the motions of two bodies). The lorry is faster at first and approaches the car. The distance decreases as long as the lorry is faster and starts to grow when the car is faster, so it is at its smallest when the velocities are equal. If the distance is then positive, the lorry does not draw level.

Formula

$x_\text{A} = v_0\,t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2$ and $x_\text{L} = x_{0\text{L}} + v_\text{L}\,t$. Equal velocities: $v_0 + a\,t_1 = v_\text{L}$, so $t_1 = \dfrac{v_\text{L} - v_0}{a}$ and $d_\text{min} = x_\text{A}(t_1) - x_\text{L}(t_1)$

Conversions

$81\,\text{km/h}$$= \dfrac{81 \cdot 1000\,\text{m}}{3600\,\text{s}}$
$= 22.5\,\text{m/s}$

Substitution

$t_1$$= \dfrac{22.5\,\text{m/s} - 15\,\text{m/s}}{1.25\,\text{m/s}^2}$
$= 6.0\,\text{s}$

$$ \begin{aligned} x_\text{A} &= 15\,\text{m/s} \cdot 6.0\,\text{s} + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1.25\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (6.0\,\text{s})^2 \\ &= 90\,\text{m} + 22.5\,\text{m} \\ &= 112.5\,\text{m}\text{;} \end{aligned} $$$$ \begin{aligned} x_\text{A} &= 15\,\text{m/s} \cdot 6.0\,\text{s} \\ &+ \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1.25\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot (6.0\,\text{s})^2 \\ &= 90\,\text{m} + 22.5\,\text{m} \\ &= 112.5\,\text{m}\text{;} \end{aligned} $$

$x_\text{L}$$= -40\,\text{m} + 22.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 6.0\,\text{s}$
$= 95\,\text{m}$
$d_\text{min}$$= 112.5\,\text{m} - 95\,\text{m}$
$= 17.5\,\text{m}$

Answer

The lorry does not draw level with the car. The distance is at its smallest, about $18\,\text{m}$, at $6.0\,\text{s}$, two significant figures like the distance $40\,\text{m}$ and the velocities.

Reasonableness

At the end of the lane ($12.0\,\text{s}$) the lorry is at $$ \begin{aligned} &-40\,\text{m} + 22.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 12.0\,\text{s} \\ &= 230\,\text{m} \end{aligned} $$ and the car at $270\,\text{m}$, so the gap is $40\,\text{m}$ again, as it should be: the areas between the velocity graphs before and after $6.0\,\text{s}$ are equal. The meeting equation $$ \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,t^2 + (v_0 - v_\text{L})\,t - x_{0\text{L}} = 0 $$ has no real solutions, because the discriminant $$ (-7.5)^2 - 4 \cdot 0.625 \cdot 40 < 0\text{.}$$

d)

Find

why the lorry catches up with a car that accelerates uniformly only to $72\,\text{km/h}$, and why it is worth accelerating to the speed of the traffic

Principle

The displacement is the area of the $(t,v)$ graph, and the distance between two bodies changes by the area between their two graphs (chapter Comparing the motions of two bodies).

Model answer

This car is slower than the lorry for the whole lane, because its greatest velocity $72\,\text{km/h} = 20\,\text{m/s}$ is smaller than the lorry's $22.5\,\text{m/s}$. In the $(t,v)$ graph the lorry's horizontal line is above the car's rising line all the time, so the lorry's displacement grows more than the car's at every moment and the gap of $40\,\text{m}$ shrinks all the time. When the gap has been closed, the lorry is level with the car and goes past. A calculation confirms this (the car accelerates uniformly over the whole length of the lane): the acceleration lasts $$ \begin{aligned} 2 \cdot 270\,\text{m} : (15\,\text{m/s} + 20\,\text{m/s}) &\approx 15.4\,\text{s} \end{aligned} $$$$ \begin{aligned} &2 \cdot 270\,\text{m} : (15\,\text{m/s} + 20\,\text{m/s}) \\ &\approx 15.4\,\text{s} \end{aligned} $$ and the car's average velocity is $17.5\,\text{m/s}$, so the lorry travels on average $5\,\text{m/s}$ faster and makes up $5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 15.4\,\text{s} \approx 77\,\text{m}$, more than the gap of $40\,\text{m}$; and in that time the lorry gets to $$ \begin{aligned} &-40\,\text{m} + 22.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot 15.4\,\text{s} \\ &\approx 307\,\text{m}\text{,} \end{aligned} $$ past the car before the end of the lane ($270\,\text{m}$). The car must then slow down and merge behind the lorry. When the car accelerates to the speed of the traffic, it is faster than the lorry in the final stage, the distance grows again and the car can merge safely in front of the lorry.

Common mistakes

  • The velocities are not converted to $\text{m/s}$, and kilometres per hour are multiplied by seconds.
  • In part a the distance is divided by the final velocity ($270\,\text{m} : 30\,\text{m/s} = 9.0\,\text{s}$), although the velocity grows gradually.
  • In part c the meeting condition is solved and the negative discriminant is taken for a calculation error, although it tells you that there is no meeting.
  • In part c the $-40\,\text{m}$ is forgotten from the lorry's position, or the initial distance $40\,\text{m}$ is taken as the smallest distance.

Also accepted

  • In part a first solving $a = \dfrac{v^2 - v_0^2}{2\,\Delta x}$ and then $t = \dfrac{v - v_0}{a}$, or using the equation of position and the equation of velocity together.
  • In part c the distance $$ \begin{aligned} d(t) &= x_\text{A} - x_\text{L} \\ &= 40\,\text{m} - 7.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot t + 0.625\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot t^2 \end{aligned} $$$$ \begin{aligned} d(t) &= x_\text{A} - x_\text{L} \\ &= 40\,\text{m} - 7.5\,\text{m/s} \cdot t \\ &+ 0.625\,\text{m/s}^2 \cdot t^2 \end{aligned} $$ and its smallest value from the vertex of the parabola.

5. The rear-window heater 15 points

Example data.

The rear window of a car has a heater with $10$ thin conducting strips side by side. At the edges of the glass there are two busbars, and each strip runs from one busbar to the other. The length of a strip is $1.10\,\text{m}$ and its width $3.0\,\text{mm}$, and the resistivity of the strip material is $3.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$. When the engine is running, the voltage between the busbars is $13.5\,\text{V}$, and a clamp meter on the lead of the heater shows $12.0\,\text{A}$. Assume that the resistance of the strips does not depend on temperature.

  1. a) What is the power of the heater, and what is the resistance of one strip? 4 p
  2. b) How thick is a strip? 4 p
  3. c) One strip breaks because of a scratch. How do the current and the power of the heater change, and does the glass warm up as well as before where the strips are intact? 3 p
  4. d) The engine is switched off, and the heater runs on the battery alone. The emf of the battery is $12.6\,\text{V}$ and its internal resistance $0.020\,\Omega$. What is the power of the heater now, when all the strips are intact? 4 p

Skills from the chapters: Resistors in series and in parallel, Resistance and Ohm's law, Electric power and the heating of a resistor, Electromotive force and internal resistance

Show the model answer and marking Open only when your own solution is ready.

Model answer

a)

Given

$10$ strips, voltage between the busbars $U = 13.5\,\text{V}$, current of the heater $I = 12.0\,\text{A}$

Find

the power $P$ of the heater and the resistance $R_1$ of one strip

Principle

Each strip runs from one busbar to the other, so the strips are in parallel: the voltage across all of them is the same $13.5\,\text{V}$, and identical strips share the current equally (chapter Resistors in series and in parallel). The power of the heater is the product of the voltage and the current (chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor), and the resistance of a strip is the ratio of its voltage to its current (chapter Resistance and Ohm's law).

Formula

$P = UI$, $I_1 = \dfrac{I}{10}$ and $R_1 = \dfrac{U}{I_1}$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$P$$= 13.5\,\text{V} \cdot 12.0\,\text{A}$
$= 162\,\text{W}$
$I_1$$= \dfrac{12.0\,\text{A}}{10}$
$= 1.20\,\text{A}$
$R_1$$= \dfrac{13.5\,\text{V}}{1.20\,\text{A}}$
$= 11.25\,\Omega$

Answer

The power of the heater is $P \approx 162\,\text{W}$ and the resistance of one strip $R_1 \approx 11.3\,\Omega$, three significant figures like the voltage and the current.

Reasonableness

The resistance of the whole heater is $\tfrac{11.25\,\Omega}{10} = 1.125\,\Omega$, and $\tfrac{13.5\,\text{V}}{1.125\,\Omega} = 12.0\,\text{A}$, as measured. A power of a few hundred watts is enough to melt thin frost off the glass in a few minutes.

b)

Given

resistance of a strip $R_1 = 11.25\,\Omega$ (part a), length $l = 1.10\,\text{m}$, width $b = 3.0\,\text{mm}$, resistivity $\rho = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m}$

Find

the thickness $h$ of a strip

Principle

A strip is a conductor of uniform thickness, whose resistance is proportional to its length and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area (chapter Resistance and Ohm's law). The cross-section of a strip is a rectangle whose area is the width times the thickness.

Formula

$R_1 = \rho\,\dfrac{l}{A}$, so $A = \dfrac{\rho l}{R_1}$, and $A = bh$, so

$h$$= \dfrac{A}{b}$
$= \dfrac{\rho l}{R_1 b}$

Conversions

$3.0\,\text{mm} = 3.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}$ and finally $1\,\text{m} = 10^{6}\,\mu\text{m}$

Substitution

$$ \begin{aligned} A &= \dfrac{3.0 \cdot 10^{-7}\,\Omega\cdot\text{m} \cdot 1.10\,\text{m}}{11.25\,\Omega} \\ &= 2.933 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{m}^2 \end{aligned} $$ and

$h$$= \dfrac{2.933 \cdot 10^{-8}\,\text{m}^2}{3.0 \cdot 10^{-3}\,\text{m}}$
$= 9.78 \cdot 10^{-6}\,\text{m}$
$= 9.78\,\mu\text{m}$

Answer

The thickness of a strip is $h \approx 9.8\,\mu\text{m}$, that is, about a hundredth of a millimetre, two significant figures like the resistivity and the width.

Reasonableness

A conducting strip printed on the surface of the glass is a very thin film, so a thickness of micrometres is credible. If the strip were a millimetre thick, its resistance would be a hundred times smaller, and the current would be too large.

c)

Find

how the current and the power change when one strip breaks, and whether the glass warms up as well as before where the strips are intact

Principle

The voltage across parallel branches is the same, so a broken branch does not change the voltage or the current of the other branches (chapter Resistors in series and in parallel). The current entering the busbars is the sum of the currents of the branches (chapter Kirchhoff's laws and potential in a circuit).

Model answer

No current passes through the broken strip any more, but the voltage across the nine intact strips is still $13.5\,\text{V}$. The current of each intact strip is still $1.20\,\text{A}$ and its power $13.5\,\text{V} \cdot 1.20\,\text{A} = 16.2\,\text{W}$, so they heat the glass as before.

The current of the heater decreases to $9 \cdot 1.20\,\text{A} = 10.8\,\text{A}$, and the power to $13.5\,\text{V} \cdot 10.8\,\text{A} \approx 146\,\text{W}$, so both decrease by a tenth. A narrow band remains where the broken strip is, and there the frost melts only with the heat of the neighbouring strips. If the strips were in series, one break would switch off the whole heater.

d)

Given

resistance of a strip $R_1 = 11.25\,\Omega$ (part a), $10$ strips in parallel, emf of the battery $E = 12.6\,\text{V}$ and internal resistance $R_\text{s} = 0.020\,\Omega$, power of the heater with the engine running $162\,\text{W}$ (part a)

Find

the power $P$ of the heater on the battery alone

Principle

Ten identical parallel strips are equivalent to one resistor whose resistance is a tenth of the resistance of a strip (chapter Resistors in series and in parallel). The internal resistance of the battery is in series with the heater, so the current is set by their combined resistance (chapter Electromotive force and internal resistance). The power of the heater is obtained from its current with Joule's law (chapter Electric power and the heating of a resistor).

Formula

$R = \dfrac{R_1}{10}$, $I = \dfrac{E}{R + R_\text{s}}$ and $P = RI^2$

Conversions

The quantities are in base units, so no conversions are needed.

Substitution

$R$$= \dfrac{11.25\,\Omega}{10}$
$= 1.125\,\Omega$
$I$$= \dfrac{12.6\,\text{V}}{1.125\,\Omega + 0.020\,\Omega}$
$= 11.00\,\text{A}$
$P$$= 1.125\,\Omega \cdot (11.00\,\text{A})^2$
$= 136.2\,\text{W}$

Answer

The power of the heater is $P \approx 136\,\text{W}$, three significant figures like the emf $12.6\,\text{V}$.

Reasonableness

The terminal voltage is $$ \begin{aligned} 12.6\,\text{V} - 0.020\,\Omega \cdot 11.0\,\text{A} &= 12.38\,\text{V}\text{,} \end{aligned} $$$$ \begin{aligned} &12.6\,\text{V} - 0.020\,\Omega \cdot 11.0\,\text{A} \\ &= 12.38\,\text{V}\text{,} \end{aligned} $$ and $\tfrac{(12.38\,\text{V})^2}{1.125\,\Omega} \approx 136\,\text{W}$, the same result. The power is about $84\,\%$ of that with the engine running, because the power is proportional to the square of the voltage and $\left(\tfrac{12.38}{13.5}\right)^2 \approx 0.84$. With the engine running the alternator keeps the voltage higher.

Common mistakes

  • In part a the resistance of the whole heater $\tfrac{13.5\,\text{V}}{12.0\,\text{A}} = 1.125\,\Omega$ is taken as the resistance of a strip, or the strips are assumed to be in series, so that the resistance is divided by ten in the wrong direction.
  • In part b millimetres are not converted into metres, or the cross-sectional area is taken as $\pi r^2$, although the strip is flat.
  • In part c it is assumed that the current of the other strips increases, because the total current is “shared” between nine.
  • In part d the internal resistance is forgotten, or the power is calculated with the emf, $\tfrac{(12.6\,\text{V})^2}{1.125\,\Omega} = 141\,\text{W}$.

Also accepted

  • In part d the power from the terminal voltage $P = \tfrac{U^2}{R}$, once $U = E - R_\text{s} I$ has been calculated.
